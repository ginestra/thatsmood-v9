*PRODA · Lead UX Designer · April 2023 – May 2026*

## The challenge

When I joined PRODA, there was no dedicated UX team.

Introducing one was a start, but there wasn't a clear vision or strategy for what design should contribute, how it would work with other teams, or where its priorities should lie.

As the team grew, requests started coming from everywhere: Marketing, Engineering, senior management. Even hackathon T-shirts.

UX was becoming an on-demand service rather than an integral part of product development. And there was little consideration for the time needed to research problems, explore solutions and evaluate decisions.

## My approach

My argument was fairly simple: reducing friction in how teams collaborated should help reduce friction in the product itself.

Better decisions, fewer avoidable problems, less rework. And, potentially, more time to improve the product rather than constantly fixing it.

**Quality and speed shouldn't necessarily be competing priorities.**

Taking time to evaluate decisions might feel slower in the moment. But repeatedly fixing avoidable problems isn't particularly efficient either.

## What I did

**Built the team.** Starting as the sole designer, I recruited two colleagues and worked to establish a more structured UX practice.

**Changed the conversation.** Before improving collaboration, I had to help people understand what UX actually involved. That meant challenging the idea that design was simply about producing interfaces, setting realistic expectations, and encouraging Product and Engineering to involve designers in defining problems, not just solving them.

**Connected design and development.** Improved visibility of ongoing work, encouraged shared design discussions, collaborative usability sessions and more accessible feedback.

**Established a shared design language.** Developed and encouraged adoption of a design system, from foundational styles to reusable components, supporting more consistent decisions across the product.

## What changed

**Better collaboration.** Developers became more involved in design decisions, and Product began involving UX earlier in some initiatives, although not consistently.

**More consistent UI.** The adoption of shared design foundations and reusable components contributed to a noticeable reduction in interface inconsistencies.

**More coherent user journeys.** We made progress towards aligning interaction patterns and pathways across the product.

These were observed improvements rather than formally measured outcomes. I don't have the data to demonstrate reductions in rework or customer support queries, so I wouldn't claim those results.

But communication improved, design became less isolated, and there was a clearer understanding of what UX could contribute.

## What I took away

Building a design team is one thing. Establishing why it exists, how it contributes and how it works with everyone else is another.

We made progress, even when delivery pressures made it difficult to sustain every change.

**Quality isn't necessarily what slows development down. Repeatedly fixing avoidable problems can be just as expensive.**
